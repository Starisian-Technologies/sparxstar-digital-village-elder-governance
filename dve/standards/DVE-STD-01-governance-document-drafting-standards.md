## 1. Purpose and Scope

### 1.1 Purpose

1.1.1 This document establishes the drafting standards applicable to all documents in the AIWA, Starisian, and DVE governance document series. It is a writing reference, not a governance authority document. It does not modify the authority model defined in DVE-FRM-01.

1.1.2 This ecosystem spans constitutional governance, stewardship policy, AI governance, rights administration, technical infrastructure standards, and contributor-facing procedures. These document types have different appropriate writing traditions. Writing all of them in the same register produces inconsistency and, in some cases, a tone that undermines credibility.

1.1.3 The target reader for any document in this series may be a Gambian government official, a WIPO or UNESCO adviser, an institutional investor, a community leader who speaks English as a second language, or a software infrastructure engineer. The drafting standard that works for all of them is: precise, measured, operational, and structurally confident.

### 1.2 Scope

1.2.1 This standard applies to:

(a) All documents drafted for or reviewed within this repository;
(b) All documents drafted by AI tooling on behalf of AIWA, Starisian, or DVE.

## 2. The Writing Traditions This Ecosystem Draws From

### 2.1 Model Overview

2.1.1 No single writing tradition covers this ecosystem. The following six traditions each inform a different layer of the document corpus. Drafters should identify which tradition is most relevant to the document type they are producing before beginning.

### 2.2 Governance Charter Writing

2.2.1 **Best model**: Mozilla Foundation governance documents; Wikimedia Foundation governance language; Internet Society and W3C governance frameworks.

2.2.2 **Why**: These frameworks are mission-driven, rights-aware, infrastructure-aware, and institutional without being corporate. They address communities of contributors and stewards, not customers.

2.2.3 **Apply to**: AIWA-CONST series, AIWA stewardship language, governance principles, contributor dignity language.

2.2.4 The target register is "digital public infrastructure stewardship," not "startup terms and conditions."

### 2.3 AI Governance Writing

2.3.1 **Best model**: NIST AI Risk Management Framework; OECD AI Principles; Partnership on AI governance papers; Hugging Face governance standards.

2.3.2 **Why**: These frameworks are operationally realistic, modular, technically literate, and layered. They handle ambiguity without becoming vague.

2.3.3 **Apply to**: AIWA-POL dataset governance documents; DVE governance framework; AI model governance; provenance and auditability requirements.

2.3.4 This style is already the natural trend of DVE-FRM-01. Maintain it.

### 2.4 Rights Administration Writing

2.4.1 **Best model**: Creative Commons licensing frameworks; collective management organisation (CMO) structures; open-source foundation licensing language.

2.4.2 **Why**: These frameworks maintain precision around the separation between ownership and administration, handle modular permissions cleanly, and address large contributor communities with plain-language transparency.

2.4.3 **Apply to**: Contributor rights documents; dataset permission frameworks; rights administration procedures; licensing governance.

### 2.5 Technical Governance Writing

2.5.1 **Best model**: IETF RFCs; ISO governance standards; CNCF and Linux Foundation standards documents; Kubernetes Enhancement Proposals.

2.5.2 **Why**: These frameworks separate policy from implementation clearly, define terms before using them, handle layered architecture through modular documents, and achieve operational precision without legal prose.

2.5.3 **Apply to**: DVE-STD series; DVE-CTRL series; DVE-PROC series; technical governance mappings between AIWA policy tiers and Starisian infrastructure tiers.

### 2.6 Cultural Governance Writing

2.6.1 **Best model**: UNESCO cultural heritage frameworks; CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance; Traditional Cultural Expression (TCE) governance papers; Indigenous data sovereignty frameworks.

2.6.2 **Why**: These frameworks ground governance in collective stewardship, community authority, anti-extraction principles, and provenance integrity. They treat communities as governance participants, not data subjects.

2.6.3 **Apply to**: AIWA-POL cultural stewardship documents; cultural restriction enforcement sections; community governance authority provisions; TCE classification and protection.

### 2.7 Intercompany Agreement Writing

2.7.1 **Best model**: Cloud infrastructure master service agreements; enterprise data governance agreements; research consortium agreements — stripped of aggressive venture commercial tone.

2.7.2 **Why**: This ecosystem requires agreements that are institutionally durable, governance-aware, and respected by parties with different national legal systems. Overly commercial language undermines institutional trust.

2.7.3 **Apply to**: AIWA–Starisian interoperability agreements; governance delegation instruments; licensing arrangements.

## 3. Style by Document Category

### 3.1 Quick-Reference Style Table

3.1.1 The following table establishes the target register for each document category:

| Document Category | Series | Target Register | Primary Writing Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constitutional charter | AIWA-CONST | Principled and concise | Governance charter (§2.2) |
| Governance policy | AIWA-POL, STAR-POL | Enforceable and operational | AI governance (§2.3) |
| Rights administration | AIWA-POL-01, AIWA-POL-02 | Rights-precise and contributor-aware | Rights administration (§2.4) |
| Cultural stewardship | AIWA-POL-03 | Community-centred and anti-extraction | Cultural governance (§2.6) |
| Governance framework | DVE-FRM | Structural and authoritative | AI governance + charter hybrid |
| Technical standards | DVE-STD | Technical and modular | Technical governance (§2.5) |
| Technical controls | DVE-CTRL | Operational and precise | Technical governance (§2.5) |
| Procedures | DVE-PROC | Workflow-oriented | Technical governance (§2.5) |
| Intercompany agreements | Joint | Legally precise but restrained | Intercompany agreement (§2.7) |
| Contributor-facing documents | AIWA-DOC | Plain language | Rights administration (§2.4) |

### 3.2 AIWA Documents: Institutional Stewardship Language

3.2.1 AIWA governance documents should sound like an institution that takes its stewardship obligations seriously and has thought carefully about them. Not like a manifesto and not like a software startup's terms of service.

3.2.2 Sentences should be:

(a) Short enough to be unambiguous in translation;
(b) Present-tense where describing ongoing obligations or system behaviour;
(c) Active-voice where assigning responsibility;
(d) Free of hedging qualifiers that weaken the obligation.

3.2.3 The correct register is closer to:

> "AIWA administers contributor rights in accordance with Gambian law and applicable international stewardship standards."

than to:

> "AIWA is the sovereign and perpetual guardian of all creative expression within its jurisdiction."

### 3.3 Starisian Documents: Technical Infrastructure Language

3.3.1 Starisian governance documents define infrastructure authority and operational constraints. They should read like a mature infrastructure company's internal governance policy: precise, modular, and technically literate.

3.3.2 Starisian documents should:

(a) Describe technical capabilities in terms of what they do, not their significance;
(b) Avoid conflating infrastructure custody with governance authority;
(c) Reference AIWA policy requirements by document ID and clause, not by paraphrase.

### 3.4 DVE Documents: Governance-Integrated Systems Architecture Language

3.4.1 DVE documents occupy a unique register. They must be simultaneously legible to governance audiences and technically credible to infrastructure audiences. They bridge AIWA governance intent and Starisian infrastructure capability.

3.4.2 DVE documents should read like governance-aware infrastructure documentation — not marketing copy, not philosophy essays, not legal contracts. The closest analogy is a well-written W3C specification combined with a governance framework: structured, operational, and institutionally grounded.

## 4. Prohibited Patterns

### 4.1 Theatrical Intensifiers

4.1.1 The following patterns produce theatrical grandiosity rather than governance authority. They must be avoided in all documents in this series:

| Prohibited pattern | Why problematic | Preferred alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "This is an absolute requirement." | Intensifier — adds no information | Restate the requirement clearly; specificity implies priority |
| "sovereign authority" | Overreaches jurisdictional precision | "governance authority" or name the specific power |
| "without exception" | Usually inaccurate and legally fragile | Define the specific obligation; address exceptions in separate clauses |
| "perpetual governance" | Creates false permanence; unenforceable | State the retention or survival clause precisely |
| "binding across all systems" | Vague scope claim | Identify the specific systems and the specific obligation |
| "political protection instrument" | Conflates governance and political rhetoric | Describe the specific protection mechanism |
| "at all times and in all circumstances" | Usually inaccurate; weakens documents when exceptions arise | State the specific scope of the obligation |
| "the paramount/supreme authority" | Grandiose title — undermines authority claims | Describe the specific authority; let the structure demonstrate hierarchy |

4.1.2 The test is: does removing this phrase reduce the legal or operational content of the clause? If not, remove it.

### 4.2 Prohibited Terminology in AIWA and DVE Documents

4.2.1 The following terms are prohibited in AIWA and DVE governance documents:

| Prohibited term | Reason | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| "de-identified data" | Imprecise; implies irreversibility that may not hold | "pseudonymized" or "identity-dissociated" with specific definition |
| "user" (for community contributors) | Reduces institutional relationship to software relationship | "contributor", "community representative", or "submitter" |
| "platform" (to mean AIWA) | SPARXSTAR is the platform; AIWA is the institution | Refer to AIWA as "AIWA", "the stewardship authority", or "the governance authority" |
| "service" (to describe AIWA's role) | AIWA is a stewardship authority, not a service provider | "stewardship", "governance authority", "administration" |
| "data subjects" (for contributors) | Reduces contributors to GDPR data-processing objects | "contributors" or, where specifically addressing data rights, "data principals" |

4.2.2 These prohibitions apply to all documents issued under AIWA or joint AIWA–Starisian authority.

### 4.3 Institutional Conflation

4.3.1 The following conflations are prohibited in all documents:

(a) Any phrasing that suggests AIWA owns or operates Starisian technology;
(b) Any phrasing that suggests Starisian has governance authority over AIWA's classification decisions;
(c) Any phrasing that treats DVE as an independent governance authority;
(d) Any phrasing that treats SPARXSTAR as a governance entity.

4.3.2 The test is: could the sentence be read as assigning AIWA's authority to Starisian, or Starisian's authority to AIWA? If yes, revise.

## 5. Structural Requirements

### 5.1 Document Header

5.1.1 Every document must open with the proprietary licence comment block:

```
<!--
Copyright (c) 2026 Starisian Technologies and AI West Africa.
All rights reserved. Proprietary and confidential.
CONFIDENTIAL · PATENT PENDING
-->
```

5.1.2 Every document must include a metadata table with the following fields: Document ID, Version, Status, Classification, Date, Issuing Authority, Cross-Reference.

5.1.3 Status values are: `Draft`, `Active`, `Normative`, `Superseded`.

### 5.2 Numbering Convention

5.2.1 All substantive provisions use the hierarchical decimal numbering convention: section number, subsection number, clause number (e.g. 3.2.1). This convention:

(a) Enables precise cross-referencing between documents;
(b) Enables unambiguous citation in agreements and legal correspondence;
(c) Signals institutional seriousness to governance audiences.

5.2.2 Headings use `##` (section), `###` (subsection). Clause numbers appear at the start of each paragraph within the subsection.

### 5.3 AIWA Document Footer

5.3.1 Every document issued under AIWA authority must close with:

```

*AI West Africa is a Busumbala Born, Gambian Grown Company.*

*Copyright (c) 2026 AI West Africa. All rights reserved.*
```

5.3.2 Documents issued under joint AIWA–Starisian authority include both copyright lines:


## 6. Terminology Standards

### 6.1 Defined Terms

6.1.1 The following terms have specific definitions within this document series. Use them consistently and do not substitute synonyms:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Contributor | A person who submits a work to AIWA through an authorised intake channel |
| Work | An intellectual creation fixed in tangible form, submitted to AIWA |
| Folklore | Expression of community cultural heritage with no identifiable individual author, transmitted through generations |
| TCE | Traditional Cultural Expression — protected under Gambian law |
| Governed artifact | A work that has entered the DVE governed pipeline |
| Sieve | The Mḗh₁n̥s governance enforcement gate |
| QUARANTINE | The DVE holding state for payloads that fail governance evaluation — not deletion |
| Release Receipt | The GovernanceToken issued by Mḗh₁n̥s when a payload clears the Sieve |
| ArtifactGovernanceDeclaration | The permanent, immutable governance record attached to every governed artifact |
| Personal Policy Token | The short-lived contributor governance preference instrument minted by Sky Esu at release |
| Group Policy | The standing community governance authority declaration, senior to Personal Policy |

6.1.2 When introducing a defined term for the first time in a document, spell it out with the abbreviation or short form in parentheses: "Traditional Cultural Expression (TCE)". Subsequent uses may use the short form.

### 6.2 Writing About AI

6.2.1 When writing about AI systems or AI processing within this governance context:

(a) Do not anthropomorphise AI systems ("the AI decides");
(b) Assign governance authority to institutions (AIWA, Starisian, DVE) not to systems;
(c) Describe AI systems by their function: "model", "processing operation", "inference system";
(d) Do not use "AI" as a vague concept. Specify: "AI training operation", "AI inference", "AI model".

## 7. Quick-Reference Checklist

### 7.1 Before Submitting a Document

7.1.1 Use the following checklist before finalising any document in this series:

**Structure**
- [ ] Licence comment block is present and correct
- [ ] Metadata table is complete (ID, Version, Status, Classification, Date, Issuing Authority, Cross-Reference)
- [ ] Sections use `##`, subsections use `###`, clauses use decimal numbering (1.1.1)
- [ ] All cross-references are accurate — document IDs and clause numbers have been verified
- [ ] Appropriate closing footer is present

**Tone and Language**
- [ ] No theatrical intensifiers (see §4.1)
- [ ] No prohibited terminology (see §4.2)
- [ ] No institutional conflation (see §4.3)
- [ ] Present tense used for ongoing obligations and system behaviour
- [ ] Active voice used for responsibility assignments

**Institutional Separation**
- [ ] AIWA is described only as governance/stewardship authority
- [ ] Starisian is described only as infrastructure/technical authority
- [ ] DVE is described only as enforcement operational stack
- [ ] SPARXSTAR is described only as platform/infrastructure layer

**Patent Sensitivity**
- [ ] No technical implementation details for Patent Family C components
- [ ] Patent-sensitive concepts referenced by name only (ArtifactGovernanceDeclaration, Sieve, Personal Policy Token, GovernanceToken)
